Ramsey model

The planner maximizes , where . At , utility is .

At zero tax, this is the planner Euler equation. With a capital income tax, it describes a decentralized household facing an after-tax net return. Tax receipts return as lump-sum transfers, so aggregate resources remain unchanged. The lecture develops the tax example with ; the dashboard also permits exogenous population growth under the same per-capita weighting. Following the lecture's dated Euler equation, the tax in period t governs the return linking t to t+1.

The second locus is concave because Euler depends on next period's capital. The labeled stable saddle path selects a unique initial consumption given predetermined capital. Nearby paths obey the same Euler and resource equations but miss the stable arm. The consumption experiment changes by up to 8% and does not snap the trajectory back onto that arm. In an unanticipated experiment, the same proportional surprise adjustment as on the stable path is applied at the shock date; nearby paths remain away from the stable choice.

Unanticipated policy changes cause consumption to jump when policy takes effect. Anticipated policy is announced at t = 0: the dashboard solves the pre-policy resource and Euler equations with a terminal condition on the new stable arm. Consumption adjusts before implementation, with no artificial jump at implementation. The nullclines and direction field change only at the effective policy date. The stationary stable arm before implementation is a reference; the announced transition need not lie on it.

The TVC diagnostic uses the stable continuation and the discounted-utility restriction , rather than claiming that a finite chart proves an infinite-horizon limit. Too little initial consumption gives excess capital accumulation and violates the TVC. Too much initial consumption eventually exhausts capital, so the path is infeasible. At positive tax, the stable allocation is an equilibrium, not the undistorted planner optimum. At 100% tax, the interior steady state disappears and the boundary solution is explicitly a finite-horizon approximation.

Saving and the saving rate are endogenous and can be negative. Utility is instantaneous utility per person , using actual per-capita consumption ; the chart does not show cumulative welfare.

The Solow model

Choose Initial to set the original economy and After shock to change parameters permanently at the shock date. Use Play, one-period step, or the time slider to follow the transition. Presets start an animation just before the shock. Use ↗ beside any graph to enlarge it, or hide the control panel to give the charts more room.

Golden rule. The purple curve and marker on the main graph use the active regime’s technology, growth, depreciation, and capital share, with saving set to α. Purple references in the small graphs show kGR, cGR = (1 − α)f(kGR), and iGR = α f(kGR). Aggregate references multiply these by the actual AtNt. When a shock changes the golden-rule target, the reference can jump; this is a benchmark, not an attainable jump in capital. The Golden rule button toggles the purple benchmark on or off in all graphs without changing the saving rate.

Factor prices. rt is the gross rental rate of physical capital before depreciation, and wt is the wage per worker.

Presets. The No growth and Growth buttons apply to the selected parameter panel. Growth sets γ = 1.02 and n = 1.01, equivalent to 2% technology growth and 1% population growth per period. Shock presets start from the initial parameters and change one value: depreciation rises by 5 percentage points, or the capital share rises by 10 percentage points (capped at each slider maximum).

Convergence speed. λ = 1 − g′(k̄) is the fraction of the capital gap closed per period, locally near the active regime’s steady state. The local half-life is ln(1/2) / ln(1 − λ). These are local approximations, not a promise about the full nonlinear transition.

Growth notation. Following the lecture, γ = At+1/At and n = Nt+1/Nt are gross factors. They remain constant within each regime. The levels At and Nt accumulate over time. No growth means γ = n = 1.

Initial conditions. A₀ = N₀ = 1, equivalently log A₀ = log N₀ = 0. Zero levels would make intensive quantities undefined. Initial capital is positive; “At steady state” sets k₀ to the initial economy’s steady state.

Timing. At the shock date, capital, technology, and labor levels are predetermined. Consumption and saving respond immediately; capital changes the next period. A growth-rate shock changes subsequent growth, without a jump in the technology or labor level.

The graphs. The main graph puts kt+1 on the vertical axis and kt on the horizontal axis, with equal scale. The amber arrows alternate between the law of motion and the 45° line. The faint curve is production f(k). In the time paths, the dotted continuation is the future, and the gray dashed curve is the no-shock counterfactual.

Saving is not a stock. The saving rate s is dimensionless. The bottom-left chart shows intensive saving/investment it = s yt. The golden-rule saving rate is s = α: increasing saving above this can reduce steady-state consumption.

Based on Tobias Broer, Macro I, “Key Facts and the Solow Model,” particularly slides 30–44 and 50. θ is an optional, constant capital-augmenting level; θ = 1 recovers the lecture specification. All time paths are levels, not deviations or stochastic impulse responses.
